# Task 5 – Research Report: Social Engineering Attacks  

---

## 📖 Introduction

Social engineering is one of the most common cybersecurity threats because it targets **people rather than only technology**. Instead of directly attacking a firewall or exploiting complex software vulnerabilities, attackers often manipulate people into making unsafe decisions.

Attackers may use **trust, urgency, fear, curiosity, or attractive offers** to convince someone to share information, click a link, open a file, or provide access.

This shows that cybersecurity is not only about technical tools. **Employees are also an important part of an organization's security.**

This report focuses on four common social engineering attacks:

- 📧 Phishing
- 👤 Pretexting
- 💾 Baiting
- 🤝 Quid Pro Quo

---

# 1. 📧 Phishing

## How It Works

Phishing is an attack where a fake email, message, website, or phone call is made to appear as if it comes from a trusted source.

The attacker may try to convince the victim to:

- Click a malicious link
- Open an attachment
- Share a username or password
- Provide personal or financial information

Phishing messages may appear to come from a bank, company, teacher, colleague, or IT department.

### Common Types

- **Spear Phishing** – Targets a specific person or small group.
- **Whaling** – Targets senior executives or important employees.
- **Vishing** – Phishing through phone calls.
- **Smishing** – Phishing through SMS or text messages.

## Real-World Example

In **2011, RSA Security** was targeted by a spear-phishing attack. Employees received emails containing a malicious attachment. One employee opened the attachment, which helped attackers gain access to the company's network and information related to its SecurID authentication system.

This incident showed that even a major security company can be affected when one employee is successfully targeted.

## Impact

- Credential theft
- Financial fraud
- Malware infection
- Unauthorized access
- Loss of sensitive information

## Prevention

1. 🔐 Enable **Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)**.
2. 📩 Use **SPF, DKIM, and DMARC** for email security.
3. 🎓 Provide regular phishing awareness training.
4. 🔎 Check links and sender addresses carefully.
5. ☎️ Verify unusual requests through another trusted communication method.

---

# 2. 👤 Pretexting

## How It Works

Pretexting involves creating a **false but believable situation** to gain someone's trust.

An attacker may pretend to be:

- IT support
- A manager
- A bank employee
- A service provider
- Another company employee

The attacker then uses the story to convince the victim to provide information or perform an action.

## Real-World Example

The **2020 Twitter attack** is a well-known example. Attackers contacted Twitter employees while pretending to be members of the company's IT support team. They claimed to be dealing with a VPN-related issue and used the situation to obtain employee information and access internal tools.

The incident demonstrated that a believable story can sometimes be more effective than a highly technical attack.

## Impact

- Unauthorized system access
- Stolen credentials
- Exposure of company information
- Financial fraud
- Compromise of internal systems

## Prevention

1. 🪪 Verify the identity of people requesting sensitive information.
2. ☎️ Use official contact numbers for callback verification.
3. 🔑 Never share passwords with anyone claiming to be IT support.
4. 📋 Follow proper procedures for account and access changes.
5. 👀 Question unusual requests, even when they sound believable.

---

# 3. 💾 Baiting

## How It Works

Baiting uses **curiosity, rewards, or attractive offers** to encourage someone to take an unsafe action.

A common example is an unknown USB drive left in a workplace or public area. Someone may connect it to a computer because they want to find out what is inside or identify its owner.

Baiting can also happen online through:

- Fake free software
- Suspicious downloads
- Fake offers
- Malicious files

## Real-World Example

A **2016 academic study** tested how people responded to unknown USB drives. Researchers placed USB drives in different locations and found that many people picked them up and connected them to computers.

The study showed how **curiosity and the desire to help** can influence people's security decisions.

## Impact

- Malware infections
- Device compromise
- Unauthorized access
- Data theft
- Spread of malware across an organization

## Prevention

1. 🚫 Never connect an unknown USB device to a company computer.
2. 💻 Restrict or control removable media where possible.
3. 🛡️ Use endpoint security and antivirus software.
4. 🔍 Scan approved removable devices before use.
5. 🎓 Train employees about physical and digital baiting.

---

# 4. 🤝 Quid Pro Quo

## How It Works

**Quid pro quo** means "something for something." In social engineering, an attacker offers a service, benefit, or help in exchange for information or access.

For example, an attacker may pretend to be a technical support worker and offer to fix a computer problem. They may then ask for login details or access to the device.

The attack works because people naturally tend to trust someone who appears to be helping them.

## Impact

- Credential theft
- Unauthorized access
- Malware installation
- Information leakage
- Employee account compromise

## Prevention

1. 🏢 Use official IT support channels.
2. ☎️ Verify unexpected support calls.
3. 🔑 Never provide passwords to anyone.
4. 💻 Do not allow unknown people to access devices remotely.
5. 🎫 Use an official help-desk or ticketing system.

---

# 📊 Comparison of Social Engineering Attacks

| Attack Type | Target | Main Technique | Possible Impact | Main Protection |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Phishing** | Employees and users | Fake messages and websites | Credential theft and malware | MFA and awareness |
| **Pretexting** | Employees | False identity or story | Unauthorized access | Identity verification |
| **Baiting** | Device users | Curiosity or reward | Malware infection | USB and endpoint controls |
| **Quid Pro Quo** | Employees | Offering help or benefit | Credential theft | Official support channels |

---

# 🔐 Key Security Recommendations

Organizations can reduce social engineering risks by combining **employee awareness with technical security controls**.

- 🎓 Conduct regular security awareness training.
- ⚠️ Teach employees to recognize urgency, fear, unusual requests, and fake identities.
- 🔐 Enable MFA for important accounts.
- ☎️ Verify sensitive requests through a second communication channel.
- 💾 Never connect unknown USB devices.
- 📋 Keep security policies simple and easy to follow.
- 🚨 Make it easy for employees to report suspicious activity.
- 📧 Use email filtering and endpoint security tools.
- 👥 Regularly review employee access permissions.

> **Important:** Employees should not feel embarrassed about reporting a suspicious message or mistake. Quick reporting can help security teams respond before a small incident becomes a major security problem.

---

# 🛡️ Key Takeaways

- Social engineering mainly targets **human behavior**.
- Phishing uses deceptive messages to obtain information or access.
- Pretexting uses believable false situations to build trust.
- Baiting uses curiosity or rewards to encourage unsafe actions.
- Quid pro quo offers help or benefits in exchange for information.
- Employee awareness is an important part of cybersecurity.
- MFA, verification procedures, training, and endpoint security can reduce risk.
- Early reporting can help prevent a small incident from becoming a major breach.

---

# 📝 Conclusion

Social engineering attacks are dangerous because they take advantage of **normal human behavior**. People naturally trust others, want to help, and sometimes act quickly when they are under pressure.

Phishing, pretexting, baiting, and quid pro quo use different approaches, but they have the same basic goal:

> **To convince a person to perform an action that benefits the attacker.**

Strong cybersecurity therefore requires more than firewalls and antivirus software. Organizations need **well-trained employees, strong authentication, proper verification procedures, secure policies, and effective technical controls**.

When employees understand how social engineering works and know what to do when something looks suspicious, they become an important part of the organization's security defense.
