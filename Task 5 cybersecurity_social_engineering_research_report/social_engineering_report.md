# Task 5 Research Report: Social Engineering Attacks

---

## Introduction

Social engineering is one of the most common cybersecurity threats because it targets **people rather than only technology**. Instead of trying to break through a firewall or exploit a complicated software vulnerability, attackers often try to convince a person to make a mistake.

They may create a sense of urgency, pretend to be a trusted employee, offer something attractive, or use fear and curiosity to influence their target. For example, an attacker might pretend to be from the IT department and ask an employee to verify their account details.

This shows why cybersecurity is not only about technical tools. **Employees also play an important role in protecting an organization.** The most common social engineering attacks include phishing, pretexting, baiting, and quid pro quo.

---

## 1. Phishing

### How it works

Phishing is an attack where a person receives a fake email, message, website, or phone call that appears to come from a trusted source. The goal is usually to make the victim click a link, open an attachment, or provide sensitive information such as a username or password.

Phishing attacks are effective because the message may look like a normal request from a bank, company, teacher, colleague, or IT department.

Common types include:

* **Spear Phishing** – A targeted attack against a particular person or small group.
* **Whaling** – Phishing that specifically targets senior executives or important employees.
* **Vishing** – Phishing carried out through phone calls.
* **Smishing** – Phishing messages sent through SMS or text messaging.

### Real-world example

In **2011, RSA Security** was targeted by a spear-phishing attack. Employees received emails containing a malicious attachment. One employee opened the attachment, which helped attackers gain access to the company's network and information related to its SecurID authentication system.

This incident showed that even a large security company can be affected when a single employee is successfully targeted.

### Impact

* Credential theft
* Financial fraud
* Malware infections
* Unauthorized access
* Loss of sensitive information

### Prevention

1. Enable **Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)**.
2. Use email security standards such as **SPF, DKIM, and DMARC**.
3. Provide regular phishing awareness training.
4. Check links and sender addresses before responding.
5. Verify unusual requests through another trusted communication method.

## 2. Pretexting

### How it works

Pretexting is based on creating a **false but believable situation**. The attacker pretends to be someone the victim is likely to trust, such as an IT employee, manager, bank representative, or service provider.

The attacker then uses the story to convince the person to provide information or perform an action. Unlike a simple phishing message, pretexting can involve a longer conversation where the attacker slowly builds trust.

### Real-world example

The **2020 Twitter attack** is a well-known example. Attackers contacted Twitter employees while pretending to be members of the company's IT support team. They claimed to be dealing with a VPN-related issue and used the situation to obtain employee information and access internal tools.

The attack demonstrated how a believable story can sometimes be more effective than a highly technical attack.

### Impact

* Unauthorized system access
* Stolen credentials
* Exposure of company information
* Financial fraud
* Compromise of internal systems

### Prevention

1. Verify the identity of people requesting sensitive information.
2. Use official contact numbers for callback verification.
3. Never share passwords with anyone claiming to be IT support.
4. Follow proper procedures for account and access changes.
5. Train employees to question unusual but believable requests.

---

## 3. Baiting

### How it works

Baiting uses **curiosity, reward, or interest** to encourage someone to take an unsafe action.

A common example is an unknown USB drive left in a public or workplace area. Someone may connect it to a computer because they want to find out what is inside or identify its owner.

Baiting can also happen online through fake free software, suspicious downloads, or other attractive offers that may contain malicious content.

### Real-world example

A **2016 academic study** tested how people respond to unknown USB drives. Researchers placed USB drives in different locations and found that many people picked them up and connected them to computers. The study showed that curiosity and the desire to help someone can influence people's security decisions.

### Impact

* Malware infections
* Device compromise
* Unauthorized access
* Data theft
* Spread of malware across an organization

### Prevention

1. Never connect an unknown USB device to a company computer.
2. Restrict or control removable media where possible.
3. Use endpoint security and antivirus software.
4. Scan approved removable devices before use.
5. Train employees about physical and digital baiting.

---

## 4. Quid Pro Quo

### How it works

The term **quid pro quo** means "something for something." In social engineering, the attacker offers a service, benefit, or help in exchange for information or access.

For example, an attacker may call an employee and pretend to be a technical support worker. They may offer to fix a computer problem and then ask the employee for login details or other sensitive information.

The attack works because people naturally tend to trust someone who appears to be helping them.

### Impact

* Credential theft
* Unauthorized access
* Malware installation
* Information leakage
* Compromise of employee accounts

### Prevention

1. Use official IT support channels.
2. Verify unexpected support calls.
3. Never provide passwords to anyone.
4. Avoid allowing unknown people to access devices remotely.
5. Use an official help-desk or ticketing system.

## Comparison of Social Engineering Attacks

| Attack Type      | Target              | Main Technique             | Possible Impact              | Main Protection           |
| ---------------- | ------------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------------------- | ------------------------- |
| **Phishing**     | Employees and users | Fake messages and websites | Credential theft and malware | MFA and awareness         |
| **Pretexting**   | Employees           | False identity or story    | Unauthorized access          | Identity verification     |
| **Baiting**      | Device users        | Curiosity or reward        | Malware infection            | USB and endpoint controls |
| **Quid Pro Quo** | Employees           | Offering help or benefit   | Credential theft             | Official support channels |

## Key Security Recommendations

Organizations can reduce social engineering risks by combining employee awareness with technical security controls.

* Conduct regular **security awareness training**.
* Teach employees to recognize urgency, fear, unusual requests, and fake identities.
* Enable **MFA** for important accounts.
* Verify sensitive requests through a second communication channel.
* Never connect unknown USB devices.
* Keep security policies simple and easy to follow.
* Make it easy for employees to report suspicious activity.
* Use email filtering and endpoint security tools.
* Regularly review employee access permissions.

Most importantly, employees should not feel embarrassed about reporting a suspicious message or mistake. **Quick reporting can help security teams respond before a small incident becomes a major security problem.**

---

## Conclusion

Social engineering attacks are dangerous because they take advantage of normal human behavior. People naturally trust others, want to be helpful, and sometimes act quickly when they are under pressure. Attackers use these behaviors to their advantage.

Phishing, pretexting, baiting, and quid pro quo attacks all use different approaches, but they have the same basic goal: **to convince a person to do something that benefits the attacker**.

Strong cybersecurity therefore requires more than firewalls and antivirus software. Organizations need **well-trained employees, strong authentication, proper verification procedures, secure policies, and effective technical controls**. When employees understand how social engineering works and know what to do when something looks suspicious, they become an important part of the organization's security defense.
